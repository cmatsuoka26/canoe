ULU

A starchy fruit loved by the Native Hawaiians. It grows on trees across the islands.

Ulu Overview

‘Ulu, or breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis), is another highly important crop in Native Hawaiian culture, valued for its productivity, versatility, and cultural meaning. Introduced by Polynesian voyagers, ‘ulu became a key staple in the traditional Hawaiian diet due to its ability to produce large, starchy fruits that could feed many people. A single tree can yield hundreds of fruits in a year, making it a symbol of abundance and sustainability. Unlike annual crops, ‘ulu is a perennial tree that can live for decades or even centuries, reflecting the Hawaiian emphasis on long-term stewardship of the land.

Agriculture and use

Native Hawaiians cultivated ‘ulu in both coastal and upland areas, often in mala (dryland gardens) alongside other important canoe plants like mai‘a (banana) and kalo. The tree required relatively little maintenance once established, and its deep roots helped stabilize soil and conserve moisture. The fruit was usually baked, roasted, or cooked in the imu (earth oven), and could be eaten like poi or cooked into stews. Its flesh is rich in carbohydrates and could be preserved by fermenting and storing it in underground pits—a valuable technique that provided food security during droughts or famine. Even the wood of the ‘ulu tree was used to make tools, drums, surfboards, and canoes, showing the plant’s importance beyond food.

Ulu and culture

Culturally, ‘ulu represented growth, resilience, and nourishment. In some Hawaiian traditions, the god Kū transformed himself into an ‘ulu tree to feed his family during a time of famine, demonstrating the plant’s role as a life-giver. Communities would gather to harvest and share ‘ulu, reinforcing values such as laulima (working together) and lokomaikaʻi (generosity). The practice of planting an ‘ulu tree when a child was born symbolized care for future generations and a commitment to providing for the community.

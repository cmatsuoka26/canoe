KO

The sweet perennial grass that dominated Hawaii's economy.

Ko Overview

Ko, or sugarcane (Saccharum officinarum), was a significant and versatile plant in Native Hawaiian culture, valued for food, medicine, ceremony, and agriculture. Brought to Hawai‘i by Polynesian voyagers, ko thrived in warm, sunny environments and could grow remarkably tall. While today sugarcane is mostly associated with plantation-era history, Native Hawaiians cultivated many traditional varieties—over 40 types—each with different flavors, colors, and uses. The sweet juice of ko made it a natural source of energy and a prized treat long before the arrival of processed sugar.

Agriculture and use

In daily life, Native Hawaiians chewed on the fibrous stalks of ko to enjoy its sweetness or used its juice to sweeten other foods such as ‘awa (kava) or medicinal mixtures. Ko was also used in cooking, especially in the imu (earth oven), where sugarcane stalks were placed on hot stones to create steam and add sweetness to food. Beyond flavor, ko had many medicinal purposes. The juice was used to soothe sore throats, ease coughing, and help digestion. Mixed with other plants, it acted as a carrier to improve the taste and effectiveness of herbal medicines.

Ko and culture

Culturally, ko held symbolic importance. It often represented growth, vitality, and prosperity. Some varieties were used in religious or ceremonial practices, including offerings to gods and the honoring of chiefs. Because sugarcane reproduced easily from cut stalks, it symbolized lineage and continuity, connecting generations through cultivation. Ko was also planted as a windbreak or boundary marker in mala (gardens), and its tall stalks helped protect other crops from the elements.

KALO

The staple of the Native Hawaiian diet. It is still grown all across the islands in lo'i kalo.

Kalo in myth

Kalo, known scientifically as Colocasia esculenta and more commonly as taro, is one of the most important plants in Native Hawaiian culture. It is considered both a staple food and a sacred symbol, deeply intertwined with Hawaiian identity, spirituality, and daily life. In Hawaiian tradition, kalo is regarded as an ancestor—Hāloa, the eldest son of the gods Wākea (Sky Father) and Ho‘ohōkūkalani (Earth Mother). According to legend, Hāloa was stillborn and buried, and from his resting place grew the first kalo plant. His younger brother, also named Hāloa, became the first Hawaiian, making kalo not just food, but family. This story establishes a profound relationship of respect and care between people and the land.

Agriculture and use

Kalo was the foundation of the traditional Hawaiian diet, providing sustenance through its starchy corm (the underground root). The plant was typically cultivated in lo‘i kalo, or irrigated terraces, which used a sophisticated system of canals (ʻauwai) to direct water from streams through the fields. These systems reflected Native Hawaiians’ deep understanding of ecology and sustainable agriculture—ensuring that water flowed continuously, nourishing both the kalo and the surrounding ecosystem before returning clean to the stream. Every part of the plant was used: the corm was cooked and pounded into poi, a smooth, slightly fermented paste that remains a cultural and ceremonial food today; the leaves (lu‘au) were cooked for stews; and the stems were replanted to perpetuate the crop.

Kalo in society

Beyond nourishment, kalo played a central role in Hawaiian values and social structure. Working together in the lo‘i reinforced laulima (cooperation) and mālama ʻāina (care for the land). Poi was shared communally, symbolizing unity and equality—everyone, regardless of status, would eat from the same bowl. Through these traditions, kalo came to embody the Hawaiian worldview: that humans are caretakers, not owners, of the land and that health and balance flow from harmony between people, place, and ancestors.
